Simplifying how technical college students understand their transfer credits and pathways to UVU.

PROBLEM

Transfer information existed, but it was buried in long, college-by-college lists full of unfamiliar terms.

WHAT I DID

Audited the existing site, interviewed 8 potential students, and redesigned the page into a guided finder that shows only the credits and degrees that apply to each student.

Project Overview

UVU's transfer page had the right information, but students had a hard time taking action.

UVU's Tech College Transfer website helps students understand how education from a Utah technical college can apply toward a degree at UVU. But the process involves a lot of information: colleges, programs, credits, course equivalents, and degree options. That makes it easy for students to lose track of what applies to them.

The Problem

Too much scrolling, too many unfamiliar terms, and no clear next step.

Navigation
Students had to work through multiple pieces of information before understanding which transfer pathway applied to them.
Clarity
Transfer credits, UVU degree options, and course information needed to be easier to scan and understand.
Next steps
Even after finding relevant information, the experience needed a clearer way to guide students toward what to do next.

What the website currently looks like

Discovery

First, I went through the site as if I were a transfer student.

I started by reviewing the existing Tech College Transfer experience from a student's perspective. I looked at how information was organized, how students moved between colleges and programs, whether important transfer information was easy to understand, and what action the site encouraged them to take next.

3 focus areas

Finding a pathway
Could a student quickly identify their technical college and find the program that applied to them?
Understanding information
Once they found their program, was it clear what credits transferred and how those credits connected to UVU?
Knowing what comes next
After reviewing their transfer information, did students have an obvious next step?

The audit gave me a few early pain points, but those were still based on my own assumptions. Before making design decisions, I wanted to see whether real users were experiencing the same problems.

User Interviews

Were students actually getting stuck where I thought they were?

Rather than designing around my own assumptions, I interviewed 8 potential users to see where their experience matched what I'd noticed, and where it didn't.

What I expected

The page felt like a lot to take in at once. There was a large amount of scrolling, long sections of text, and transfer terminology that wasn't always easy to understand. Even after finding the relevant information, I was still unsure what a student was supposed to do next.

What users told me

The interviews confirmed many of those concerns. Users also felt there was too much scrolling, had trouble understanding what the information meant, and weren't always sure where the experience was leading them.

What this told me

The solution wasn't just to shorten the page. The information needed to be broken into smaller, more intentional steps. Instead of showing everything at once, the experience could guide students through a series of choices, showing them only the information relevant to their college, program, and transfer path. It also needed to make the next step much more obvious once they reached their results.

One thing I didn't expect was that users actually wanted more opportunities to click and make choices. They felt it would be easier to navigate if they could select what applied to them instead of scrolling through everything on one page.

Design Direction

I decided to guide students through a few simple steps.

Before designing screens, I defined the path a student should be able to follow from the moment they arrive on the page to the point where they understand what comes next.

Once the student flow was defined, I used it to rethink the page structure. The sitemap helped me decide what information needed to appear first, what could support the transfer finder, and where students should be guided afterward.

With the journey and page structure defined, I started exploring how the experience could work visually.

Early Wireframes

We led with visuals to make the page more interesting to explore.

My first direction focused on breaking the experience into clearer sections, reducing the amount of information shown at once, and giving students more obvious actions throughout the page.

  1. We added a stats section near the top to give students more context about the transfer program and reinforce that this was an established pathway.
  2. The graphic designer and I wanted the page to feel less text heavy, so we explored using each technical college's logo as a visual selection card, an idea we later moved away from.
  3. Once a college was selected, its available programs would appear below.

Iteration

Jakob's Law made me rethink the way users made selections.

As the design became more refined, I revisited some of the interaction choices from the first wireframes.

  1. I replaced the college carousel with dropdown menus based on Jakob's Law: users are more comfortable with patterns they already recognize from other websites. Dropdowns made choosing a college and program feel more familiar and straightforward.
  2. Not everyone visiting the site would already know their college or program, so I added a "Just exploring?" option that lets them browse without completing the guided flow.
  1. After students enter their information, the page moves directly to their matching transfer options, so they don't have to search through the rest of the page.
  2. Transfer results can be expanded or collapsed, allowing students to quickly compare their options.
  3. Once students understand their options, the experience leads into a form so they can take the next step and connect with someone for help.

The iteration focused on making the experience feel familiar while only showing students what they needed, when they needed it.

Final Design

Now students can find their transfer credits without scrolling through everything else.

The final design uses familiar interaction patterns, personalized results, expandable transfer details, and clear next steps. Students can find the information that applies to them without sorting through everything on the page.

  1. Students choose their technical college and program using familiar dropdowns.
  2. The results page surfaces only the degrees and credits connected to their selections.
  3. Expandable sections keep detailed course information available without overwhelming the page.

What's Next

The next step is seeing how students use the new page.

The redesign is currently being developed. Once it's live, I plan to run usability tests with technical college students to see whether the new experience solves the problems we found in research. I'll focus on the same three areas from my audit:

  • Finding a pathway: Can students choose their college and program without help?
  • Understanding information: Do they understand which credits transfer and how they apply to a UVU degree?
  • Knowing what comes next: After seeing their results, do they know how to take the next step?

Reflection

This project changed how I think about simplifying complex information.

This project taught me that simplifying an experience doesn't always mean removing information. Sometimes it means deciding when and how that information appears. Through research, feedback, and iteration, I learned to rely more on familiar interaction patterns and design around the decisions users actually need to make.
