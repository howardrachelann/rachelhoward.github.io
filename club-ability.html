Club Ability Website Redesign

Redesigning the website for a bilingual nonprofit that teaches technology to families of all ages.

PROBLEM

Club Ability had grown to serve adults and families, but its website still felt like a kids' program.

WHAT I DID

Interviewed the founder and 6 potential users, ran eye-tracking tests with 6 participants, then reorganized the site around three clear paths for different audiences.

OUTCOME

Presented to Club Ability's founder as a set of research-backed design recommendations.

Context

Club Ability makes sure language and income never stand between a family and tech education.

Club Ability is a nonprofit making tech education accessible to underserved families. With bilingual programs ranging from digital literacy for kids to AI workforce training for adults, they ensure language and income are never barriers to opportunity.

The Problem

Club Ability wanted to reach all ages, but the website still spoke mostly to kids.

Audience
The experience currently feels more tailored to children, making it less effective for engaging parents and adult learners.
Navigation
Too many tabs, unclear labels, and unexpected content placement made important information harder to find.
Hierarchy

Research

I combined interviews with eye tracking to understand both what users said and where they looked.

I needed to understand Club Ability's future vision, how people currently navigate experiences like this, and where the existing website was creating friction.

Stakeholder interview1 founder
I spoke with founder and executive director Juliette Bautista to understand the organization's goals for the redesign.
Audience interviews6 participants
I spoke with potential users about what they expect when visiting an unfamiliar website, how they respond when information is difficult to find, and what helps them feel confident taking the next step.
Eye tracking test6 participants · iMotions
I used eye tracking to observe where users focused their attention, what they missed, and how they visually navigated the existing website. This helped identify areas where hierarchy and important information weren't coming through clearly.

Findings

Users couldn't tell who the site was for, or where anything was located.

The site felt primarily kid-focused

One participant immediately said the site looked like it was for kids, and described it as a site for parents signing children up. Multiple participants also struggled to find an adult learner's perspective because most visible testimonials felt child focused.

→ The redesign needed to preserve Club Ability's current youth audience while making adults and parents feel more intentionally represented.

Finding key information required too much work

Participants did not always know where information would live. One user could not find course pricing, another expected course images to lead to sign-up information, and adult testimonials were difficult to locate because users were unsure which section would contain them.

→ Navigation labels, content placement, and interactive cues needed to align more closely with what users naturally expected.

Important content needed stronger hierarchy

The eye-tracking sessions showed that users relied heavily on prominent imagery, navigation labels, and familiar interaction patterns to decide where to go next. One participant spent significant time scanning the homepage and navigation, while another repeatedly clicked elements that appeared interactive but led nowhere.

→ The redesign needed clearer visual priorities and more predictable interactions so users could understand what mattered and what they could click.

Defining the direction

How might I help every visitor quickly understand what Club Ability offers, find what they need, and take action?

Information Architecture

I organized the site around the three things visitors came to do.

I organized the site around the actions Club Ability wanted visitors to take most: learn about the organization, explore and enroll in programs, and get involved through volunteering or donations. These goals became the three main navigation paths: About Us, Programs, and Get Involved.

Wireframing

With the new structure in place, I started designing how users would move through them.

I turned the new site structure into wireframes for the homepage, programs, and navigation. The goal was to make it immediately clear where different audiences could begin, while keeping important information and actions easy to scan.

Home page

Clear audience entry points help parents, adults, and students quickly find where to start, while Club Ability's mission and community are brought forward.

Programs

I organized programs into simple age-based filters and surfaced key details directly on each card. This makes it easier for users to scan their options and find a program that fits their needs.

Mobile navigation

The mobile menu groups content into clear audience and action-based paths, making programs, involvement, contact, and donation options easier to find quickly.

Final Design

Parents, adults, and students can now find their way in from the homepage.

Clear audience pathways

Parents, adults, and students can quickly identify where they belong instead of navigating through the same generic experience.

Programs that are easier to scan

Age ranges, pricing, and key program details are surfaced earlier so users can compare options without digging through pages.

Simplified mobile navigation

Content is grouped into familiar categories and action-based pathways, making important pages easier to find.

More visible ways to support Club Ability

Donation and involvement opportunities are given clearer placement instead of being buried within the site.

Reflection

Good design started with understanding who I was designing for.

This project reminded me that good design starts with empathy. It was easy to look at the original website and immediately think about what I wanted to change visually, but talking with stakeholders and potential users showed me that the bigger challenge was understanding who the site needed to serve and what each person was trying to accomplish.

I learned to pay more attention to how different audiences experience the same information. A parent, an adult learner, and someone looking to support the organization may all visit the same website with completely different needs. Designing around those perspectives helped me make decisions based less on assumption and more on what would actually make the experience clearer and more useful for people.
